01 Lesson 1

2D is sacred

So we made 3D switchable, and invisible in 2D mode.

You shouldn't be able to tell it's a 3D panel. Three families of switchable optics exist: a parallax barrier, a diffractive backlight and a liquid-crystal lens. The LC lens is the architecture that scaled. Liquid crystal fills the interstitial region between a polymer lens and the cover glass, so it works on any display underneath.

02 Lesson 2

3D must stay sharp

So we track your eyes instead of spraying resolution across views.

You don't want to see the pixels. Some resolution loss is inherent to how we see 3D, and the theoretical limit is a 2× linear loss. The job is to stay as close to that limit as possible.

Multiview (lightfield) panels spread resolution across many fixed views. Tracked stereo renders two views at full resolution and aims them at your eyes.

Every tracked-stereo product was received better than the multiview ones before it. Next: switching 3D pixel by pixel, from fast global switching with time multiplexing to per-pixel local switching.

03 Lesson 3

3D must be comfortable

So we hold the bar on calibration and predictive tracking.

What makes people queasy

  • A new visual signal, like a new pair of glasses
  • Static ghosting
  • Dynamic ghosting
  • Accommodation–vergence conflict

What fixes it

  • Calibration first: a per-pixel ghosting map from the factory
  • Precise eye location at presentation time. XY is easy; Z is less forgiving on large panels; predictive tracking masks latency
  • Correction embedded in the interlacing shader, with no rendering penalty. It removes all ghosting except on a pure black background
  • Comfort as a layout rule: guardrails in the SDK based on viewer distance and content distance

Same 3D module, measured with and without Leia calibration and software, for static off-angle crosstalk and dynamic crosstalk. The module is identical; the software is what changes the result.

The real lesson

Software now exceeds optical complexity.

This is what customers really pay for.

04 Lesson 4

3D must be (nearly) free

So we drive nanoimprint and the supply chain toward touch-like cost.

As cheap as adding touch

  • A thin, transparent optical layer
  • Weaving firmware in the ASIC or TCON
  • One-time factory calibration

Nanoimprint lithography

A roll-to-plate process with high replication fidelity, so the cost of the lens layer scales down as glass size goes up.

05 Lesson 5

3D must be easy to make

So we make conversion automatic and the tools open: the SDK and DisplayXR.

2D to 3D conversion is the starting point

  • AI depth + view synthesis
  • Image to Gaussian splat
  • Game mods

Billions of 3D capture devices are already in the wild, and native 3D content comes from the authoring tools creators already use.

DisplayXR is a standard for 3D display content: one interface for any 3D display or headset, plus display-specific extensions. It points toward a cross-reality OS.
